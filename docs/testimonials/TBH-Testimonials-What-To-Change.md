# The Testimonials

**Testimonials only · Change list**

Fourteen changes, each with the reason it matters. Everything not about a seller's story has been removed.

> ### The one thing this document is about
>
> A homeowner reads a testimonial to answer one question: "did someone like me do this, and did it work out?" Every change below either makes that question answerable, or removes something that makes the answer untrustworthy. Nothing else is included.

| Untrue | Weak | Missing | Wrong ask |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **4** | **4** | **4** | **2** |

Based on the full conversion audit and the challenge to the design proposal, both 17 August 2026.

Scope: the Seller Stories section, the legacy video block, the composite "seller experience" blocks, and the trust bar. Nothing else.

---

## Group A — Fix what isn't true

### Four things a careful seller can catch you on

These come first because they are not opinions and they are not improvements. Each one is a statement on the site that does not survive being checked, in a category where the first question every prospect asks is "is this real?"

### 1. Remove the invented sellers

**What's there:** On the situation pages, under the heading "What Sellers Like You Experience" — "Sarah and Mark, living in Chandler, were facing a contentious divorce… closed on their Chandler home in 14 days for $530,000." The footnote admits it: "Details reflect a composite of similar situations."

**Why it has to go:** Three reasons, in order of size.

- It sits in the same scroll as Don, Brian and Alice, who are real. A visitor who reads the composite footnote has no way to tell that the section below is different in kind. The fake content contaminates the true content — which is the exact opposite of what a testimonial section is for.
- Invented names plus invented dollar figures is the specific thing the FTC rule addresses. A composite is a legitimate device; a composite with proper nouns and prices under a heading claiming this is what sellers experience is not.
- The disclaimer creates an obligation instead of removing one. It says "typical outcome" — which means you now have to be able to prove 14 days and 96% of value is typical.

**Do instead:** strip the names and the figures. Describe the pattern without pretending it's a person. Then replace the block entirely with a real story as the library fills — that version converts better anyway, because it's checkable.

### 2. Stop rewriting the seller's city

**What's there:** The legacy video block labels each seller with whatever market the visitor is browsing. Rachel sold in Ybor City, Tampa; on the Phoenix page she reads as Phoenix. Running on 31 of 32 markets. *(Found by Patrick, not by me — my first audit only looked at the homepage.)*

**Why it matters more than it looks:** On a "we buy houses in your city" page, the city is the entire claim. It is the one detail that makes the testimonial relevant, so it is the one detail you cannot get wrong. And it is self-defeating: the moment a Phoenix visitor recognises that the "Phoenix" seller is generic, every other seller on the page becomes suspect too.

**Do instead:** show the seller's real city, always. An honest out-of-market story is worth far more than a dishonest local one — and a Charlotte seller labelled Charlotte is perfectly usable in Phoenix until you have a Phoenix story.

### 3. Settle Alice or Angie — from the file, not from memory

**What's there:** The card says Alice Blackwelder. The video and thumbnail are `Angie-Malone.mp4` and `Angie-Malone.jpg`, on a public storage bucket anyone can read.

**Why:** It is almost certainly a records mix-up rather than wrong footage — but it is the first story every visitor sees, and the name attached to a testimonial is a claim about who is speaking. You cannot ship the lead story on an open question.

**Do instead:** confirm against the signed consent and the closing file. Then rename every asset to something non-identifying — `story-07-charlotte-nc.mp4` — so a seller's name never lives in a public URL again.

### 4. Remove the divorce line from the story text button

**What's there:** Every story's Text button pre-writes "Hi Tarek, I need to sell my house — going through a divorce." On all three stories. None of which is about a divorce.

**Why:** It puts a false and very personal statement into the seller's own outbound message. A relocating homeowner taps the button and watches their phone compose a claim that they're divorcing. That reads as either careless or manipulative, and it is the kind of thing that gets screenshotted.

**Do instead:** it's a template leak — the situation pages already do this correctly and dynamically ("I'm going through a divorce in Phoenix…"). Wire the story CTA to the story's own situation and the bug becomes a feature: a foreclosure story pre-writing "I'm behind on payments and need to move fast" is genuinely useful and pre-qualifies the lead.

*Related, inside the same card: the Call button shows (866) 407-3411 and dials a different number. Not strictly a testimonial issue, but it's in the story card, so fix it while you're in there.*

## Group B — Fix what's weak

### Four things the stories say that prove nothing

The test for every line in a testimonial: what would have to be false for this to be a lie? If nothing would — if the sentence cannot be wrong — it is not evidence and it should be cut. "Stress-free experience" cannot be wrong. "Closed Monday" can. Only one of those is proof.

### 5. Rebuild Alice's story, and don't let it lead

**What's there:** "A quick and fair cash sale." Body: "Alice worked with Tarek Buys Houses to sell her home with a straightforward cash offer and a smooth closing process." Bullets: Fair cash offer / Quick closing / No hassle. No situation tag at all.

**Why it's the biggest content problem:** This isn't a testimonial, it's a caption — written in the third person by a marketer, using the four most common phrases in the category. Take the name out and it could be pasted onto any competitor's site unedited. That is what a fabricated testimonial reads like, which means it drags down the credibility of the two real stories sitting next to it. And it is currently first, on every page.

**Do instead:** transcribe her actual video and build the written story from her own words. Until that's done, lead with Don.

### 6. Rewrite the headlines to name a situation and an outcome

**What's there:** The recognition trigger — "Loss in family," "Relocation" — is in 9-pixel grey tag text. The biggest text on the card says nothing.

**Why:** A homeowner decides in about five seconds whether a story is about them. Right now the largest element on the card is generic and the only useful element is the smallest. That is exactly backwards. The headline's job is recognition; the tag's job is metadata.

**Do instead** — every headline must finish this sentence for a stranger: "this is about someone who ____, and what happened was ____."

| Now | Instead |
|---|---|
| "You guys made an offer on Saturday morning and negotiated it that day" | "His father-in-law died on Friday. By Monday, the house was sold." |
| "The traditional selling process just wasn't practical for us" | "They were moving out of state and couldn't wait for a buyer — so they sold, then stayed a month." |
| "A quick and fair cash sale" | Pending the transcript — but it must name her situation. |

**Banned from every headline:** quick, fair, smooth, hassle-free, seamless, great experience. Those six words are the category's wallpaper and they signal "marketing" to precisely the reader you're trying to convince.

### 7. Replace the benefit bullets with facts

**Why:** "Fair cash offer" is the seller repeating the buyer's claim. It proves nothing. Concrete language is what signals that a company was actually paying attention — and it's the difference between a compliment and evidence.

| Now | What it proves | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Fair cash offer | Nothing | "I got three offers. Theirs was highest and the only one that didn't change after the walkthrough." |
| Quick closing | Nothing, without a number | "Nine days from the first call to the wire landing." |
| No hassle | Nothing | "No showings, no cleaning, no repairs. Nobody walked through my house." |
| Stress-free experience | Nothing | Delete. Name the specific thing that removed the stress. |
| Contacted Friday / Offer Saturday / Closed Monday | Speed, weekend availability, and that the offer survived contact with reality | Keep. Make it a graphic. |
| Stayed in the home a month after closing | Flexibility, and an answer to "where do I live?" | Keep. Promote it to the headline. |

### 8. Put a date and a real city on every story

**What's there:** No dates anywhere. Brian's card says "CA" while his panel says "Stevenson Ranch, CA."

**Why:** Two different jobs. Dates prove you're still trading — Sundae's newest review is fifteen months old and the page reads like a company that stopped. Precise cities make a claim falsifiable, and a claim someone could check is a claim they believe. "Stevenson Ranch" is more credible than "CA" precisely because it's more specific.

**Do instead:** month + year and the actual city, on the card and in the panel, matching.

## Group C — Fix what's missing

### Four things that aren't there at all

### 9. Get more stories — this is the whole project, not a side task

**Where you are:** three stories. The site is built around seventeen situations — divorce, foreclosure, inherited, behind on payments, major repairs, relocating, downsizing, vacant, tax liens, bad tenants, code violations, fire damage, hoarder house, bankruptcy, job loss, medical bills, tired landlord. Your stories cover two of them.

**Why this outranks every design decision:** you have built 544 pages that each promise "we understand your specific situation," and then show a Charlotte cash sale with no situation attached. The gap between the promise and the proof is the entire problem. No layout fixes it. Patrick's proposal names this correctly — "one generic clip against seventeen situations is the constraint on every option in this document" — and then schedules it as a parallel task. It isn't parallel. It's the critical path.

> **The mechanism**
>
> Ninety seconds of vertical phone video at the closing table. One question: "What was going on with the house before you called us?" Peak relief, peak detail, zero recall decay. Standing policy from the next closing. At your volume that produces more real story inventory in one quarter than any competitor in this category has accumulated in years.

Then work the backlog: pull the last 150 closings, flag everyone who left a good review, call 40 of them. Prioritise by which situations get the most ad spend. Target 15 stories in 30 days, 25 in 60.

*Ask eight questions and let people finish. The one that matters most: "What made you skeptical about us?" — the answer pre-handles the exact objection the next reader is holding. And "Did you get other offers? How did ours compare?" — nothing else you publish will answer the lowball fear.*

### 10. Get the three stranded videos into the library

**What's there:** Rachel, Diane & Kim, and Sara exist — but only in the legacy block, which the admin panel can't reach. They aren't in the testimonials database at all.

**Why it's the fastest win available:** it takes you from one video to four with no filming. That's a 4× content gain this month, and it's the difference between a section that looks alive and one that looks abandoned.

**Do instead:** migrate all three with their real locations before the legacy block is retired — not after. And check whether each has burned-in captions; someone has to watch all three on mute.

### 11. Make the trust bar clickable and show the counts

**What's there:** "4.7 Google · A+ BBB · 1,000+ Homes Purchased" — static text. Not links, no review count, no date.

**Why, three separate reasons:**

- Unclickable badges push people off your site. Users try to click them, fail, then go search the platform manually — and you've just sent high-intent traffic to Google to read whatever it finds.
- A rating with no count means nothing. When asked to choose, people pick 4.5 stars from 57 ratings over 5 stars from 4, roughly two to one. Volume is what makes a score credible. "4.7" alone is a number with no denominator.
- On-site testimonials are automatically discounted; live third-party feeds aren't. Everyone assumes you picked the good ones. A live feed is believed specifically because it contains mixed ratings.

**Do instead:** link both, show the count and an "as of" date. Then go further — publish the full star distribution including the one-stars, filterable. That sounds insane and it's the most credible thing anyone in this category does; We Buy Ugly Houses is the only one doing it. Purchase likelihood actually peaks around 4.0–4.7 and declines toward 5.0. A visible 4.7 with real complaints under it beats a spotless wall.

### 12. Attach real numbers to a real story

**What you already have:** a section called "How We Calculate Your Offer — no black boxes, no guessing games", walking through ARV minus repairs. It's excellent. It's attached to a made-up example.

**Why this is the biggest opportunity in the whole audit:** across every competitor I checked — Opendoor, Offerpad, Sundae, We Buy Ugly Houses, HomeLight, Clever, Orchard — I found two dollar figures in total. Nobody publishes what a seller actually got. Which means nobody answers the one question every prospect is really asking: "are you going to lowball me?" Not one word on your stories addresses it either.

**Do instead:** take Don's actual closing — real ARV, real repair estimate, real number — and run it through the calculator you already built. Be honest that a cash offer is usually below retail, and show the arithmetic: what a listing might have brought, minus four months, minus repairs, minus commission, minus carrying costs. Conceding the drawback is what makes the rest believable. This is a copy change, not a build, and it makes you the only company in the category that does it.

## Group D — Fix the ask

### Two things about what happens after someone reads

### 13. End the story with the form, not a phone call

**What's there:** the only buttons in the story card are Text and Call. On the situation pages the address form — a single field — sits two sections below, behind the FAQ.

**Why this is the single largest conversion change on the list:** you have just spent a paragraph earning a small amount of trust from someone in the middle of a divorce or a death in the family. Typing an address is a low-commitment act. Phoning a stranger to talk about it is a high-commitment act. The section spends its entire budget earning the low ask and then makes the high one.

It's also worth noticing that this decision is currently being made by default rather than on purpose — no one chose "phone call" over "form," it just happens to be what's in the component.

- **Primary:** address field + "See what we'd offer for your house"
  *"See" frames it as looking, not transacting. "We'd" is conditional — it signals nothing is triggered by clicking. Both matter at the moment someone is curious but not decided.*
- **Underneath, smaller:** "No obligation, no fee. You can say no after you see the number — most people who ask do, and that's fine."
  *That sentence is the strongest one available here. It removes the fear of being trapped, and it's a sentence a high-pressure operation couldn't write — which is exactly why it proves you aren't one.*
- **Secondary:** Call and Text stay — a real minority of this audience prefers voice. They just aren't the default.

### 14. Give every story its own URL

**What's there:** no `/seller-stories` page, no per-story links, not even an anchor on the section.

**Why it's more important than it sounds:** without a URL, a story can only ever be seen by someone who happens to scroll past it. With one:

- An ad targeting divorce sellers can land on a divorce story instead of a page that contains one.
- You can retarget based on which story someone actually read.
- Stories can rank for "sell inherited house Tampa" and the other seventeen situations you're already built for.
- A rep can text a hesitant seller the story of someone in their exact position — the highest-value use of this content, and today it's impossible.

**Worth flagging the ordering issue:** the current plan is to build situation-matching across 544 pages before the stories have addresses. That's building the routing before the destinations exist — and with three stories, the matcher has nothing to match. Permalinks first, matching once the library passes fifteen.

## Sequence

### What order to do it in

| When | Do | Why then |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Items 1–4 — invented sellers, city rewriting, Alice/Angie, divorce SMS. | None of these needs a decision from anyone. Three are one-line fixes. All are live. |
| Week 1–2 | Items 6, 8, 13 — headlines, dates and cities, form CTA. And lead with Don, not Alice. | Cheapest changes with the biggest effect. No new content required — you're re-presenting what you already have. |
| Week 2 | Item 9 — closing-table video ask goes live as standing policy. Item 10 — migrate the three stranded videos. | Capture has to start before anything else can compound. Migration is a 4× content gain with no filming. |
| Week 2–3 | Items 5, 7, 11 — rebuild Alice from her transcript, replace the bullets with facts, link the trust badges. | Needs the transcript and the review integrations, so it lands just after the quick wins. |
| Day 30–60 | Items 12, 14 — real numbers on a real deal, and `/seller-stories` with permalinks. Library to 25 stories. | Both need the library to exist first. Item 12 is where the differentiation actually lives. |

### How you'll know it worked

None of this is currently measurable, because there is no form in the section to measure against. Instrument these six before the changes ship:

| Measure | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Story → form-start rate | The only one that really matters: does reading a story make someone enter an address? |
| Form completion by entry story | Which stories carry weight. Promote the winners, replace the rest. |
| Which story gets selected | Whether the headlines are doing recognition work. |
| Video completion | If it's low, the first three seconds are the problem — not the length. |
| Situation-filter usage | Validates or kills the categories. If nobody filters, they're named wrong. |
| Lead quality by entry story | A foreclosure story producing contract-ready sellers is worth more than a relocation story producing browsers. |

Don't A/B test items 1–4 — a fabricated seller doesn't need a control group. Do test the genuinely uncertain things: CTA wording, whether publishing negative reviews helps your audience, and whether dollar figures increase or decrease form starts.

---

> ### The whole audit in one paragraph
>
> Your testimonials don't let a seller recognise themselves, don't give them anything they could check, and don't end in the thing you actually want. Meanwhile you're publishing invented sellers next to the real ones, and the best story you own — a man whose father-in-law died on a Friday, whose house was sold by Monday, who says on record that he called because of the show — is sitting in the second tab with its grammar intact and its point buried. Fix the untrue things this week, lead with Don, put the form under every story, and start filming at closings. Everything else on this list follows from those four.

*Nothing here is legal advice. Items 1, 2 and 3 touch federal testimonial rules and should go to counsel before the next deploy.*
